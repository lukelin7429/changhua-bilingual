Discipline Without Corporal Punishment

By November the honeymoon is over and you have a class you cannot settle. This module is the short version of what you are allowed to do about it — which is more than most foreign teachers assume, and narrower in one specific direction than almost anyone expects.

The one sentence

Corporal punishment is prohibited by statute, not by school policy — and it covers far more than hitting.

Article 8 of the Educational Fundamental Act (教育基本法) protects students' right to bodily autonomy and to the development of their personality, and requires that they suffer no corporal punishment or bullying. Taiwan banned corporal punishment in schools in 2006.

What counts as corporal punishment

The Ministry's guidance defines it functionally: an act done for the purpose of punishment, in which a teacher applies physical force to a student — or has the student, or a third party, do so — or requires the student to hold a particular physical position, such that the student objectively suffers physical pain or bodily or mental harm.

Read that second clause again, because it is the one that catches well-meaning foreign teachers. Press-ups as a penalty, running laps as a penalty, holding arms out, squatting, kneeling, or standing far beyond a reasonable period are all capable of being corporal punishment. Intent to be harsh is not required, and “it's just exercise” is not a defense.

What you MAY do

The permitted general measures come from the Ministry's 學校訂定教師輔導與管教學生辦法注意事項 (as amended 5 February 2024). The ones actually available to you in an English lesson:

MeasureNotes

A useful rule: if it is not on this list, do not reach for it. Collective punishment — the whole class stays in because one student misbehaved — is not on it. Neither is punishment for poor test results.

The hard limits

  • No physical force不得體罰
    Including grabbing an arm to move a student, pushing, flicking, or tapping with an object. The one exception is genuine danger, below.
  • No physical positions as penalty
    Press-ups, squats, laps, holding out arms, kneeling. This is the clause most often broken by accident.
  • No humiliation
    Respect for the student's dignity is a stated principle of the guidance. Mocking a student in front of the class, or remarks about their family or intelligence, are not discipline.
  • No punishment for grades
    Academic results are not a behavior matter.
  • Confiscation is temporary
    You may hold an item that is disrupting the lesson, but it must be returned. Anything beyond the end of the lesson goes through 學務處 — do not keep a student's phone overnight.

The emergency exception

Where there is a risk of harm to another person's life or body, a student may be physically removed from the scene, and outside help sought. The February 2024 amendment also strengthened your position in the other direction: in urgent situations, 學務處 and 輔導室 cannot refuse a teacher's request for assistance.

In practice: if a fight starts, your job is to protect the other students, call for help immediately, and not to become the person who wrestles a teenager. Send a student to the office if you have no other way to raise the alarm.

Your actual position as a foreign teacher

You generally hold no formal disciplinary authority in the school's system. That sounds like a weakness and mostly is not — what you have instead is the relationship, the seating plan, the lesson design, and a co-teacher who can say in three seconds of Chinese what would take you five minutes of English.

The escalation ladder, in order: handle it yourself in the moment (a look, a name, a seat change) → your co-teacher, during the lesson if needed → the homeroom teacher 導師, who holds the real relationship with that student and their family → 學務處 (生教組) for anything serious or repeated. Skipping straight to the office for small things spends credibility you will want later; never escalating at all is how a class becomes unteachable by March.

If you have already crossed a line

Everyone has moments. If you shouted, or grabbed a wrist, or made a class do laps before you knew the rule: tell your co-teacher the same day, in your own words, before anyone else describes it. A teacher who reports their own mistake is a colleague having a bad day. The identical event, first described by a student to a parent three days later, becomes something else entirely — and if a student appears to have been hurt, the reporting duty in Module 4 applies regardless of who caused it.

One thing to do this week

Agree a signal with your co-teacher — a word, a look — that means “take this one, I'm getting nowhere.” Deciding it in a calm week is the difference between a smooth handover and an argument in front of thirty students.
