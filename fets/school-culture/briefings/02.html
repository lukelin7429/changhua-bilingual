The trap: make-up school days 補課日

Put these in your phone today

Some Saturdays are working school days.

When a national holiday falls next to a weekend, Taiwan often bridges the gap to create a longer break — and then makes up the lost day on a Saturday. That Saturday is a normal school day: full timetable, full attendance, and you are expected to be there.

These dates are set long in advance and published on the school calendar (行事曆). Missing one because you assumed Saturday was free is the most common avoidable mistake of a first year — and the least forgivable, because the date was on a document you were given in week one.

Exam periods 段考

Each semester has two or three 段考 (periodic exams), plus a final. During exam periods the timetable is rewritten: your English class may be shortened, moved, merged, or canceled outright, sometimes with a day's notice.

The practical consequence: do not schedule anything that depends on continuity into an exam week. Ask 教務處 for the exam dates at the start of each semester and plan your units around them, not into them.

The events that eat lessons

運動會Sports day

A whole-school event, often a Saturday, with weeks of rehearsal beforehand that also take lesson time. You are expected to attend and to be visible.

校慶School anniversary

The school's birthday, sometimes combined with sports day. Performances, alumni, local dignitaries. Lessons are canceled.

校外教學Field trip

A full-day class outing. Your lesson with that class simply does not happen; you may or may not be invited along.

親師座談會Parent meeting

Parents come to school, usually early in each semester. You may be asked to appear briefly or introduce yourself — ask in advance whether you are expected.

教學觀摩Observed lesson

Colleagues watch a lesson and discuss it afterwards. This is professional routine, not an inspection or a threat — being asked is normal.

畢業典禮Graduation

June. A significant emotional event for the whole school. Attend if you taught the graduating year.

How you find out anything has changed

In order of authority: the school calendar (行事曆) is the source of truth; the LINE group is where same-day changes are announced, usually in Chinese; your co-teacher is who you ask when you cannot decode either.

Two habits worth building in your first month. Put the whole calendar in your phone on day one, including every exam period, event and make-up Saturday. And ask your co-teacher, once a week, the single most useful question available to you: “Is anything changing next week?” — that ninety seconds prevents most of what feels like being kept in the dark.

Leave, and asking for time off

Your leave comes from your contract, not from the regulations that govern Taiwanese certified teachers. Two practical rules regardless of what it says: ask early, because your classes have to be covered by someone; and never book flights before the leave is confirmed. Requests around Lunar New Year, exam weeks and the end of term are the hardest to grant — ask about those months in advance, not weeks.

One thing to do this week

Sit down with the 行事曆 and a phone. Enter every exam period, every whole-school event, and every make-up Saturday between now and June, each with a reminder the day before. It takes fifteen minutes and it removes about half of everything that goes wrong in a first year.
