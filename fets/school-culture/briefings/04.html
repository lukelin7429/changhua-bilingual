The one sentence

If you suspect a child is being harmed, tell your school's designated person immediately — and in any case within 24 hours of the moment you knew.

You do not need proof. You do not need to be right. You do not investigate. You tell someone whose job it is to act.

Why this lands on you

Article 53 of the Child and Juvenile Welfare and Rights Protection Act (兒童及少年福利與權益保障法) lists educators among the people who must report. The Domestic Violence Prevention Act (家庭暴力防治法) Article 50 does the same for suspected domestic violence. Both give the same deadline: immediately, and no later than 24 hours.

Your school will treat you as someone with this duty. Whether or not you want to argue about how the statute applies to a contract teacher, the correct action is identical either way — and it takes about ninety seconds.

What triggers the duty

Article 53 lists six categories. In plain terms, you report when you have reason to suspect a child is:

What is not your job

  • Investigating
    You are not the investigator. Social services and the school's designated staff handle that. Your own investigation can contaminate theirs.
  • Interviewing the child
    Listen if they talk. Do not question them, do not ask leading questions, do not ask them to repeat it for someone else. Repeated questioning harms both the child and the case.
  • Deciding if it is serious enough
    The threshold is suspicion, not certainty. Weighing the evidence is someone else's job, and doing it yourself is the most common way a report never gets made.
  • Telling the family
    Never contact the parents first. In a significant share of cases the person you would be warning is the person causing the harm.
  • Promising secrecy
    Do not say “I won't tell anyone.” Say: “Thank you for telling me. I have to tell one person whose job it is to help, and I will tell you who that is.”

The 24-hour clock

The clock starts the moment the first member of staff knows — and everyone who knows shares that same 24 hours. Passing it to a colleague does not reset it. If you told someone and you are not confident it went anywhere, that is your problem too, and you can report directly yourself.

You are protected, and so is the child

Article 53 makes the reporter's identity confidential by law. A report made in good faith that turns out to be wrong is not a problem — that outcome is built into the system. A report never made is.

If a child starts telling you something

Four things, in order. Stay. Do not look shocked or rush away. Listen without questioning — “mm”, “okay”, “thank you for telling me”. Be honest about what happens next: one person has to be told. Write it down as soon as you are alone — the date, the time, where you were, and their words as closely as you can remember them, not your interpretation. Then tell the designated person. Today.

Self-harm and suicide risk

If a student tells you they are hurting themselves or thinking about ending their life: do not leave them alone, do not promise to keep it secret, and get 輔導室 involved immediately — walk them there if you can. This is also a reportable event through the campus safety system. The 24-hour framing still applies, but in practice the answer is “now”, not “today”.

One thing to do this week

Ask your 學務處 who the designated person for child-protection reports is at your school, and put their name and extension in your phone next to 113. Doing that in a quiet week is the entire point of this module.
